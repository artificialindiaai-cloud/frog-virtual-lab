Extrasystole and compensatory pause

–Coupling interval (s)
–Compensatory pause (s)
–Normal cycle (s)
–Coupling + pause
Press a stimulus button. In systole nothing happens. In diastole you get an extrasystole, then a compensatory pause.
Properties of extrasystole: (1) It occurs only if the stimulus falls in diastole, because cardiac muscle has a long refractory period during systole. (2) It is weaker when earlier. (3) It is followed by a compensatory pause, because the next pacemaker beat falls in its refractory period; coupling interval + pause = 2 normal cycles. (4) The beat after the pause is stronger (post-extrasystolic potentiation). (5) Atria are unaffected, because the stimulus is applied to the ventricle. (6) Cardiac muscle cannot be tetanised.
Drug pearls: Cold ↓rate. Warm ↑rate (but ↓force). Adrenaline ↑rate and force. ACh ↓rate and force, and ↑A–V delay. Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors, so ACh and vagal stimulation no longer work. Nicotine first stimulates vagal ganglia (slowing), then blocks them and releases catecholamines (acceleration). Excess NaCl antagonises Ca²⁺ and stops the heart in diastole. Ca²⁺ → systolic arrest. K⁺ → diastolic arrest.

5. Genesis of fatigue

0%Total fatigue
Start a train at 2–5 Hz and watch the force fall.
Pearls: Two stimuli: second ignored in the absolute refractory period (about 3 ms), summation if it falls during the first twitch, separate twitches if later. After-load: the muscle first contracts isometrically until tension equals the load, so latent period rises and shortening falls as load rises. Free load stretches the muscle first and changes initial length. Work = load × shortening, so it is zero at no load and zero at maximal load. Fatigue is first at the neuromuscular junction (ACh depletion), then in the muscle (ATP/PCr fall, lactic acid, K⁺ rise).
